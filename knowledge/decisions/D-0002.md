# D-0002 — The truck is designed as a Blockbench project in code, to the reference, and reviewed as renders

**Decision**: The generator's bundle is dropped. `devtools/art/build.py` designs the truck
itself -- a list of cubes with rotations, folders and per-face decals, to the reference
image Rusty gave -- and writes it as Blockbench projects the protocol reads as saved, plus
a preview project with the wheels on and the paint tinted. Before any booth run the
preview is rendered through Blockbench's own camera from the standard angles and close
on every joint, and the renders are looked at against the reference. The profile's
numbers still come from the same constants as the cubes.

**Why**: The repaired bundle shipped as 1.0.0 and Rusty's verdict was that it looked
nothing like the reference and was low quality even by the game's standards: no glass to
see through, chests that were not chests, a cab that was a brick. Its defects were of the
kind numbers do not catch -- a windshield hanging between pillars with nothing over it, a
frame leaning forward, a dash that filled the driver's view -- and the bundle's geometry
was too far from the reference to edit toward it. Designing the cubes directly, with a
project Blockbench opens, gives a model that can be looked at and moved by hand, and a
review loop whose input is a picture.

**Consequences**: The script is the source until a hand edit lands in a project; then the
saved project is. Two conventions are pinned in the script and in Vanilla Wheels'
D-0006: a positive turn about +X carries the top toward the nose, so a rake is negative,
and texture rows count from the top. Two decoys to keep out of the review: a preview's
wheels are moved from the origin, so decals painted by world position subtract the cube's
own origin; and Blockbench's camera has a narrower field than the game's, so the dash
looms in a Blockbench driver's-eye render and the booth's first-person shot is the one
that counts for that.
